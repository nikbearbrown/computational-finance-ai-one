# Separate Asset Risk From Account Risk

The modeled asset falls by 25%. The owner's account equity falls by more than 40%. The market did not publish two different prices. The account owes fixed dollars to someone else.

These cases only model hypothetical cash and accounting claims. They are not trading instructions or live margin rules. Actual fees, settlement, lender terms, liquidity and regulatory requirements must come from the specific agreement and environment.

## Long position and a fixed debt balance

A fabricated account acquires an asset worth 1,000 using 600 equity and a 400 loan. Ignore interest and other costs. At price 750 the account equity is 750−400 = 350, a loss of 250 against initial equity 600, about 41.67%. At price 650 the account's equity is 250. Suppose a particular invented maintenance test says equity must be at least 40% of asset value, assessed immediately at each price. At 750, equity/asset value is 46.67% and passes; at 650 it is about 38.46% and fails. No statement about the terms of any actual lender follows.

Solve the boundary using the stated definition: `(P−400)/P ≥ 0.40`. For positive P this means P ≥ 400/0.60, about 666.67. The holder's cash response may depend on when funds are available. Avoid reporting the computed gap or maintenance percentage as cash automatically needed: first specify how the account would actually be changed in this model.

## Borrowed shares change what the account owes

In a separate hypothetical example, an account owes ten borrowed units. It originally sold each for 50, puts the 500 proceeds in a restricted balance, and independently contributed 200 cash. With current price 62, the liability to return those ten units is measured at 620 in this exercise. The remaining equity in this simplified account is 700−620 = 80. The loss is 120 relative to contributed cash, before fees or other costs. Opposite direction is not the same as opposite risk: one position's debt is fixed in dollars, the other liability changes with the unit price.

**Teardown.** A position summary calls the restricted sale proceeds “profit received,” then ignores the obligation to return the units. Rebuild the account with both sides and identify the location of the unrecognized loss.

**Transfer.** With the first account price 650 and the loan fixed at 400, imagine adding cash as collateral without repaying the loan. Suppose the same ratio counts collateral in both account equity and gross assets. Solve for the smallest modeled top-up to clear 40%. Compare that with using new cash immediately to repay debt with no residual account cash. Explain why these are different actions, not advice to take either one.
