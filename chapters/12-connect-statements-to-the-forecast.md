# Connect Statements to the Forecast, Not to a Headline

An analyst has a positive change in earnings and a bigger forecast of available cash. The operating model, however, gives more customers time to pay and calls for new equipment. The decision depends on which dollars can actually reach capital providers and when. Tie the accounting model before talking about price.

## Establish a consistent illustrative period

All amounts here are synthetic and in the same monetary scale. The entity has no debt or interest, and begins with: cash 60, receivables 40, inventory 70, net equipment 210, trade payables 35 and equity 345. Verify assets 380 equal liabilities plus equity. During the period sales are 300. Operating expenses total 215, including 20 of noncash depreciation. Use stipulated operating cash taxes of 25% of earnings before interest and taxes; all taxes are immediately payable in this simplified exercise. There are no other expense or capital changes beyond those listed. Equipment investment is 38.

Receivables end at 52, inventory at 66 and payables unchanged at 35. Ignore noncash translation, acquisitions or write-offs. Treat operating working capital here as receivables plus inventory minus trade payables. It starts at 75 and ends at 83: an 8 increase uses cash under the stated account movements.

## Price the right flow

Earnings before interest and tax are 85. Operating profit after the modeled taxes is 63.75. Add back 20 depreciation, subtract 38 investment and 8 increase in working capital. Cash from the modeled operations and investment before owner distributions is 37.75. One dated forecast of that amount at a 10% required return is worth about 34.32 at time zero; that is the PV of that single forecast cash flow, **not** a valuation of the entire business.

Now add a separate cash distribution to equity holders of 10, which should not reduce the operating valuation cash before distributions. Closing cash is 60 + 37.75 − 10 = 87.75. Closing net equipment is 210 + 38 − 20 = 228. Ending assets total 433.75, with payables 35 and equity 345 + 63.75 − 10 = 398.75. The model ties through a different route. A balanced statement does not prove the assumptions are right, but unexplained missing assets are a reason to stop. The [SEC overview](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/begfinstmtguide.htm) gives a broader orientation to financial statements; this teaching example sets its own simplifications and no general tax rate.

**Teardown.** A forecast adds depreciation to sales and claims that higher receivables are a cash inflow. Reverse those implied entries separately. Identify which evidence would tell an analyst whether changes in receivables arose from actual collections, reporting reclassifications, or forecast dates.

**Transfer.** If closing receivables increase to 62 without an additional sale, due only to delayed collections, rebuild the cash flow, ending cash and ending assets. Compute the new PV of the single period. Identify the forecast driver most vulnerable to a date error and one observation to verify it.
